Navigating a PhD can be challenging, but a few practices can make it easier, especially if you start early. Here are some tools that helped me structure my research and, later, write my thesis.

## literature sources

When you start your PhD, it is normal to feel lost in an ocean of papers. Simply starting to read is probably the best way to learn about your field, but it is also important to keep up with new research. At first, before you know the leading researchers in your area, try subscribing to journal newsletters or subject-specific mailing lists. These share either new articles from a journal or a selection of papers on a particular topic. The latter selections are usually more focused, though their quality depends on how carefully the list's authors curate them.

![Subscribe to mailing lists for your topic](/assets/images/posts/how-to-survive-your-phd/mailing-list.png){: width="500" }

Reading a review is a good way to get a first overview of a topic and find specific papers to follow up on. Their references can also help you discover the groups working in the area. Once you know some of the leading researchers, you can follow their groups on Twitter or [Bluesky](https://bsky.app/). By the end of my PhD, my feed was so well curated that I could keep up simply by checking what the groups I followed had shared. This can also lead you to more resources, as group leaders often share one another's publications. Many researchers also share their manuscripts without a paywall &mdash; this can be helpful if your group does not provide a subscription to a specific journal.

![Subscribe to lab accounts on twitter](/assets/images/posts/how-to-survive-your-phd/twitter.png){: width="500" }

## building your own wiki

Building my own wiki was probably the best decision I made as it greatly simplified writing my thesis. The idea is to choose one tool for your library and another for saving what you learn from your reading, then link the two.

I used [Paperpile](https://paperpile.com/) to save and read papers and highlight useful information. Paperpile requires a subscription, so if your group does not provide that, you could try a free option such as [Zotero](https://www.zotero.org/). Reference managers usually offer browser plugins that make it easy to add papers to your library. Tags, folders, and colour-coded highlights can help keep the library organised and easy to navigate (have fun creating your own system!). Reference management tools often integrate with writing software, too, and let you export the bibliography as a BibTeX file. I also recommend noting down any thoughts that pop up as you read: these might turn into an interesting project idea later.

![Build your own library](/assets/images/posts/how-to-survive-your-phd/paperpile-tags.png){: width="500" }

To bring everything together in one knowledge base, I used [Obsidian](https://obsidian.md/). It is a powerful, almost-free tool that can integrate with [Zotero](https://community.obsidian.md/plugins/obsidian-zotero-desktop-connector), among others. Obsidian has useful plugins for citations, AI tools, themes, and flashcards, and it can sync across devices through cloud services, including its [own paid option](https://obsidian.md/sync). I saved anything I wanted to remember and used internal links to connect my notes. I also cited sources using references imported from Paperpile via a cloud-hosted BibTeX file. Over time, this became my own wiki and workflow documentation. When I started writing my thesis, I could simply open my notes and find my literature research ready.

![Build your own wiki](/assets/images/posts/how-to-survive-your-phd/obsidian.png){: width="500" }

## organise your work

PhD researchers often accumulate several projects, which can become hard to manage. I recommend using a tool to track progress and prioritise tasks. [Notion](https://www.notion.com/) is one versatile option: it is free for one private user and highly customisable (including community templates). You can integrate it with other apps, use it across devices, track projects separately, customise views, and assign tasks to specific projects. I recommend writing down every task and ordering them by deadline or priority. I also found it extremely helpful to record what I had done on each task; you can add text and images to the task description. A year later, when you cannot remember exactly how you made a particular plot or solved an annoying bug that suddenly reoccurred, those notes can save you a lot of headaches. Keep track of what you have tried, including what did not work, to avoid repeating yourself.

![Keep track of your tasks](/assets/images/posts/how-to-survive-your-phd/notion-todo.png){: width="500" }

Notion can make Gantt charts to help with tight deadlines. You can add dependencies, link related tasks, and connect your private calendar. It is also helpful to store things you need to remember but do not want to keep in your head. For me as a German PhD student, that meant bureaucracy: how many lectures I needed to attend, where and when to send each document, and so on.

## bonus: save inspiration!

Finally, as someone who loves visualisation, I made a habit of saving plots I came across in papers for inspiration. This turned out to be helpful whenever I was unsure how best to present my own data.

![A collection of plots saved for inspiration](/assets/images/posts/how-to-survive-your-phd/inspo.png){: width="500" }

In the end, you do not need to use all these tools or build a perfect system from day one. Start with what helps you keep track of what you read, what you tried, and what inspires you. With time, useful practices will stick and make the rest of the PhD (and especially thesis writing) feel much more manageable.
